## TL;DR

Joshua Gans reframes AI not as a general-purpose automation technology but as a dramatic improvement in prediction. The book builds a microeconomic framework: AI reduces the cost of prediction, which reshapes decision-making, complements judgment, and restructures industries. Organized into four parts: demand for AI prediction, supply of AI inputs, pricing under different market conditions, and policy (antitrust, privacy, IP, regulation).

## Key Claims

1. **AI is prediction** — Recent advances are exclusively advances in statistical prediction techniques. This is the fundamental lens through which to understand AI's economic impact.
2. **First-order effect is on decision-making** — Prediction's primary value is improving decision efficiency, not merely automating tasks.
3. **Prediction has substitutes and complements** — Rule-based systems, data, and human judgment can substitute for or complement AI prediction. Understanding these relationships determines where AI creates value.
4. **AI supply chain matters** — Input data generation, training data curation, and model development each have distinct economic properties and bottlenecks.
5. **Pricing depends on market structure** — The price of predictions varies dramatically based on whether judgment is exogenous/endogenous and whether markets are competitive or monopolistic.
6. **Policy implications are micro, not macro** — Antitrust, collusion, privacy regulation, IP rights, and bias are the critical policy arenas, not broad employment/productivity debates.
