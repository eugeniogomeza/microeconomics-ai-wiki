# Economics of AI

**AKA:** AI economics, microeconomics of artificial intelligence, prediction economics  
**Related:** [[mit-microeconomics-ai]], [[prediction-machines]], [[joshua-gans]]

## TL;DR

The economics of AI is the study of how artificial intelligence — fundamentally understood as cheap prediction — reshapes decision-making, market structures, pricing, and policy. The core insight is that AI's first-order effect is not automation but improved prediction quality, which changes what decisions are made, how they are made, and who makes them.

## Explanation

### AI as Prediction

Joshua Gans argues that recent AI advances are exclusively advances in statistical prediction. This reframing has profound economic consequences:

- **Prediction is an input to decision-making** — Better prediction enables better decisions, not just automated ones.
- **Prediction has substitutes** — Rules, heuristics, data, and human judgment can all substitute for AI prediction.
- **Prediction has complements** — Judgment, action, data collection, and infrastructure make prediction more valuable.

### Supply and Demand

**Demand side:**
- What decisions need prediction?
- How much does prediction quality improve the decision?
- What is the willingness to pay for a marginal improvement in prediction accuracy?

**Supply side:**
- Input data generation
- Training data curation
- Model development and compute

### Pricing

The price of predictions depends critically on:
- **Market structure** (competitive vs. monopoly)
- **Whether judgment is exogenous or endogenous** — If the firm using the AI also controls judgment, pricing is different than if judgment is bought separately.
- **Negotiation dynamics** — Prediction quality changes bargaining power in negotiations.

### Policy Implications

Gans identifies six key policy domains:
1. **Market Power** — Who controls the prediction layer?
2. **Collusion** — Can AI facilitate tacit or explicit collusion?
3. **Privacy Regulation** — Data is the input; privacy rules affect supply.
4. **Intellectual Property** — Training data and model weights both raise IP questions.
5. **Misinformation** — Cheap prediction also means cheap generation of false content.
6. **Bias and Discrimination** — Predictions trained on biased data perpetuate discrimination.
